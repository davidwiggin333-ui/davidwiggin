GPS fitness watch

In progress

Board, backAdd a render or screenshot
Stage 1 breadboard prototypeAdd a photo of the breadboard build

A wrist-worn GPS and heart-rate tracker that I'm designing from the schematic up. I wanted to see the sensor data for myself, so I'm building the hardware to learn what it takes to get a clean signal from a wrist.

  • Architecture. An nRF52840 Bluetooth module (Raytac MDBT50Q-1MV2) at the core. An nPM1300 power management chip runs a 150 mAh LiPo with pogo-pin charging. A MAXM86161A optical sensor reads heart rate, a BMA400 handles motion, and a u-blox MAX-M10S GPS module is switched off between runs to save power.
  • Interface. A 1.28 in round 240 x 240 touch display, a side button, a second button for start/stop and laps, a coin vibration motor, and 8 MB of QSPI flash for run logs and watch faces.
  • Stage 1: breadboard. A Seeed XIAO nRF52840, a MAX30102 heart-rate board, and a motion-sensor board wired on a breadboard, programmed in Arduino, to see real sensor data before committing to a board.
  • Stage 2: custom PCB. Hierarchical schematic drawn in KiCad, with fabrication and assembly through JLCPCB.
  • Enclosure. Sized around 51 mm across, similar to a current sports watch, with a sensor window on the case back.
  • Next. Test how accurate the optical sensor is on the wrist, then adjust sensor placement and fit.

I replaced the car's hydraulic clutch with steering wheel paddles and a servo actuator to shorten shift time, working across mechanical, electrical, and firmware.

  • Electronics. Worked with the electronics team on a custom steering wheel PCB for clutch, shifting, and aero controls.
  • Firmware. Programmed an Arduino to read driver input and drive the clutch servo.

  • Result. About 300 ms saved per shift, checked against GoPro video and logged data.

Differential mounts

I designed and optimized the differential mounts for WashU Racing's drivetrain. The mounts had to be light, cheap, and easy to set up accurately, and the final design is 44% lighter with infinite chain adjustment through a turnbuckle.

  • Requirements. Minimize mass for faster acceleration, set chain length accurately, cut cost so the budget could go to other parts, and support an off-the-shelf adjustable differential.
  • Load analysis. Built a spreadsheet to find the maximum force on the drivetrain in different car scenarios, including an estimate of the engine's internal inertia, with a 1.5 safety factor on the sprocket load.
  • Lightweighting. Used SolidWorks FEA to take material out of the mounts wherever the loads allowed it.
  • Turnbuckle adjustment. Added a turnbuckle so the chain can be adjusted to any length in its range, not just in fixed steps, which lets the team set chain tension accurately at the car.
  • Testing. Wrote a test plan for differential settings. Testing improved cornering performance by 4%.
  • Team. I led 13 students across design, analysis, manufacturing, and testing of the powertrain and drivetrain, the biggest system on the team. We placed 44th of 120 at the international competition.

Fantasy Flat Track

A full-stack fantasy pick'em web app for American Flat Track racing, built with Next.js and Supabase (PostgreSQL) and deployed on Vercel. The game follows how race weekends really work.

  • Game. Podium-style pick screens, race-by-race pick breakdowns, global and group leaderboards, and shareable recap cards.
  • Leagues. Public and private groups with invite codes, plus a bracket format with per-match locking and auto-advancement alongside the points game.
  • Admin and data. A results workflow with draft and publish, and a schema that carries multiple seasons of race history.
  • Outreach. I pitched a racing promoter on running an official pick'em for their event, and I'm designing features around their format. Live at FantasyFlatTrack.com.
